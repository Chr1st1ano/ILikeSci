CHAPTER I

  THE PROJECT AND ITS BACKGROUND

Introduction

Science education is a fundamental component of basic education because it develops learners' critical thinking, inquiry skills, and understanding of natural phenomena. In Grades 3–6, Science subjects introduce more complex and abstract concepts such as ecosystems, force and motion, matter, energy, and Earth systems. These topics require clear explanation, visualization, and meaningful instructional strategies to ensure that learners fully understand scientific ideas.

 However, In many elementary schools, particularly those with limited instructional resources, Science instruction continues to rely primarily on textbooks, printed modules, and teacher-centered discussions.. While these approaches remain useful, they often lack interactivity and visual representation, which are essential for explaining abstract scientific concepts. As a result, teachers may find it challenging to maintain learner engagement and improve conceptual understanding. Specific problems identified in elementary Science classrooms include lack of student interest in Science, limited availability of learning materials, difficulty understanding complex concepts, and lack of experiments or hands-on activities.

With the advancement of educational technology, teachers increasingly rely on various digital instructional materials such as PowerPoint presentations, educational videos from platforms like YouTube, online simulations, and other multimedia resources to enhance classroom instruction. These digital materials help teachers present Science lessons through animations, videos, simulations, and visual demonstrations that make abstract concepts easier to understand. Related literature supports the use of technology in science instruction. Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning emphasizes that learners understand concepts better when information is presented using both words and visuals rather than text alone  (Mayer, 2019). Similarly, Clark and Mayer (2016) highlight that well-designed multimedia instruction improves learner comprehension and retention. Studies also show that digital teaching materials increase student engagement and help teachers deliver lessons more efficiently by reducing preparation time and providing ready-to-use instructional content.

This study is also aligned with the global call for quality education under the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 4: Quality Education, which promotes inclusive and equitable quality education and encourages the use of innovative teaching methods and technology to improve learning outcomes. Integrating digital instructional materials supports this goal by enhancing teacher effectiveness and improving access to quality learning resources.

In response to these educational needs and global learning goals, this study proposes the design and Development of iLikeSci: An Interactive Science Teaching Material System for Grades 3 to 6 Science Teachers at  Bay Central Elementary  School. The system is designed to support effective Science instruction through structured, multimedia-enhanced, and curriculum-based digital lessons supports the delivery and management of curriculum-aligned digital lessons based on the Department of Education MATATAG Curriculum  It will be a teacher-controlled, offline-capable Progressive Web App (PWA) that delivers interactive Science lessons with videos, animations, and gamified activities to engage students and address the identified problems of low interest, limited materials, conceptual difficulty, and lack of experiments. An additional digital recitation assessment feature will also be included to support teachers in tracking student participation and performance.

Project Context

Teaching Science subject requires effective instructional strategies that support learners' understanding of abstract and complex concepts such as matter, energy, force and motion, ecosystems, and Earth systems. In Grades 3–6, pupils are expected to develop deeper scientific thinking, which requires not only verbal explanation but also visual and interactive presentation of lessons.

At Bay Central Elementary School, Science teachers commonly utilize traditional instructional materials such as textbooks, printed modules, teacher-made PowerPoint presentations, and other standalone digital resources. While these materials support classroom instruction, they are often dispersed across different applications and lack interactivity, centralized organization, and integrated assessment features. As a result, teachers spend additional time preparing and switching between resources during lessons, while students have limited opportunities to engage with interactive activities, multimedia content, and structured assessments.  As a result, some scientific concepts remain difficult for learners to fully understand, especially those that require step-by-step demonstration or visual simulation.

Teachers also face challenges such as limited access to updated instructional materials, lack of ready-made interactive content, and time constraints in preparing engaging lesson presentations. These conditions may affect the quality of Science instruction and the level of learner engagement during classroom discussions. Specifically, the following problems have been identified: lack of student interest in Science, limited learning materials, difficulty understanding abstract concepts, and lack of experiments or hands-on activities.

The researchers conducted an interview with Ms. Coney Alcantra Quintos, a Science teacher at Bay Central Elementary School. According to Ms. Quintos, the school has televisions and projectors that teachers already use for showing videos and presentations. However, these devices are not integrated into a structured digital system that provides organized and interactive Science lessons based on the prescribed topics under the MATATAG Curriculum. Teachers currently rely on manually prepared PowerPoint presentations and printed modules, which take time to prepare and often lack the multimedia elements needed to fully engage students.

The school serves a total population of 931 students from Grades 3 to 6. Specifically, Grade 3 has 101 male and 120 female students (221 total); Grade 4 has 102 male and 124 female students (226 total); Grade 5 has 129 male and 123 female students (252 total); and Grade 6 has 107 male and 125 female students (232 total).

To better understand the challenges faced by Science teachers at Bay Central Elementary School, the researchers conducted a survey among four teachers handling Grades 3, 4, 5, and 6. The survey results confirmed that students experience difficulty in understanding Science lessons due to lack of interest, complex topics, limited instructional materials, and lack of student participation. The teachers also identified specific topics under the MATATAG Curriculum where students struggle the most, including Materials, Living Things, Force/Motion/Energy, and Earth and Space.

When asked about their teaching strategies, all four teachers reported using lecture, group activities, multimedia (videos, animations), and recitation. They rated interactive activities and digital tools as the most effective methods for improving student understanding. The teachers also expressed a clear need for a digital system that provides ready-to-use, interactive, and curriculum-aligned Science lessons that can be displayed on the school's existing television or projector.

The proposed iLikeSci system offers several improvements over the traditional PowerPoint-based instruction currently used by teachers at Bay Central Elementary School. Unlike PowerPoint presentations that require teachers to spend hours creating slides and searching for materials, iLikeSci provides ready-to-use curriculum-aligned lessons with videos, animations, and gamified activities. While PowerPoint relies on text and static images, iLikeSci offers interactive multimedia elements that encourage active student participation. In terms of recitation and fairness monitoring, iLikeSci provides digital tracking of student participation and highlights students who have not yet been called, which is not possible with manual recording. The system also works offline through PWA technology, addressing the weak internet connectivity at the school. Overall, iLikeSci reduces teacher preparation time, increases student engagement, and provides automated tracking features that are not available in traditional PowerPoint instruction.

Related literature emphasizes the importance of using multimedia and interactive teaching materials in Science education. According to Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning, learners understand concepts better when information is presented using both visual and verbal formats (Mayer, 2019). In addition, Clark and Mayer (2016) highlight that well-designed multimedia instruction improves comprehension and retention by reducing cognitive overload and enhancing meaningful learning experiences. Studies also show that web-based instructional systems can improve teaching efficiency by providing organized, accessible, and reusable learning materials.

Furthermore, the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 4 (SDG 4: Quality Education) promotes inclusive and equitable quality education and encourages the use of innovative and technology-based teaching strategies to improve learning outcomes. The integration of digital instructional systems in classroom instruction supports this goal by enhancing access to quality teaching resources and improving instructional delivery.

This system aims to provide structured, curriculum-based, and multimedia-enhanced Science lessons that teachers can use during classroom instruction to improve engagement, understanding, and overall teaching effectiveness. Project Purpose 

This study aims to develop iLikeSci, a teacher-controlled digital platform that supports Science teachers at Bay Central Elementary School in delivering interactive, multimedia-enhanced Science lessons. The system is designed to address the identified challenges in elementary Science instruction, namely: low student engagement in Science lessons, difficulty in understanding abstract scientific concepts, limited access to interactive and curriculum-aligned instructional materials , and limited opportunities for experiments or hands-on activities. 

The system will operate on a teacher's laptop and display instructional content through the school's existing television or projector, eliminating the need for students to use personal devices during classroom instruction. It will serve as a centralized instructional platform where teachers can access curriculum-aligned lessons, locally stored teaching materials such as PowerPoint presentations, PDF files, videos, spreadsheets, and other educational resources through a single interface. Selected online resources, including YouTube links, may also be integrated when internet connectivity is available. By organizing instructional materials in one platform, the system minimizes the need to switch between multiple applications, resulting in a more organized and efficient lesson delivery. The system will be developed as a Progressive Web App (PWA), allowing teachers to access it through a web browser across compatible devices without requiring installation from an app store

Project Scope and Limitation 

The iLikeSci system is designed as a teacher-controlled platform for delivering multimedia-enhanced Science lessons to students in Grades 3 to 6 at Bay Central Elementary School. The system will operate on a teacher's laptop and display content on a television or projector for the whole class to see. Students will not need personal mobile devices, as the system is intended for classroom presentation use only. The system will be developed as a Progressive Web App (PWA), allowing it to be accessed on desktop devices through a web browser without requiring installation from an app store.

The system includes a lesson delivery module that presents selected curriculum-aligned Science lessons for Grades 3 to 6 using multimedia elements such as videos, animations, and simulations. It also provides interactive visual teaching support with structured lesson flows that enhance traditional classroom instruction (including PowerPoint) by integrating interactive multimedia content. The content follows the Department of Education's MATATAG Curriculum standards for Science. The system features offline functionality, allowing limited materials to be accessed without an internet connection, with optional online synchronization for data backup. As a Progressive Web App (PWA), the system operates through a web browser and supports offline classroom use, with internet connection required only for optional online synchronization and backup. The system also includes a digital recitation assessment module that tracks student participation and recitation performance.. In addition, a fairness control feature uses a rule-based prioritization mechanism during digital recitation. It  monitors each student's participation count throughout the Science class. It tracks the number of times a student has been called for recitation and compares it with the total number of recitations conducted. When multiple students have not yet participated, the system prioritizes those students and recommends them to the teacher for the next recitation. The system does not automatically select a student; instead, it recommends students based on their participation records. The teacher retains full control over the final selection and may choose any student based on classroom needs. This decision-making process promotes equitable participation by prioritizing students who have not yet participated while maintaining teacher control over classroom management. 

There are Three types of users who interact with the system: the teacher, the system administrator, and the student. The Teacher serves as the primary user of the system, operating the platform to deliver curriculum-aligned Science lessons, facilitate interactive classroom activities, manage instructional materials, administer quizzes and digital recitations, utilize the recitation tracking feature to monitor student participation, and access assessment records; the System Administrator, who manages system setup, data backup, and user accounts. Students participate as supervised users during classroom instruction. They do not have individual accounts or independent access to the system using personal devices. Instead, they interact with the system one at a time through the teacher-operated laptop during teacher-facilitated activities such as digital recitations, quizzes and interactive learning exercises.

The developed project has the following limitations. The system is limited to selected Science topics for Grades 3 to 6 under the MATATAG Curriculum, such as those identified for Grade 4, including: chemical properties of materials, changes in materials due to temperature, human body systems, life cycles of animals, food chains, force and motion, properties of magnets, sources and uses of light, sound, and heat energy, types of soil, characteristics of weather, and the Sun's importance to life on Earth. Similar topics for Grades 3, 5, and 6 will be covered based on the corresponding curriculum standards. The system does not cover the entire Science curriculum or other subjects such as Mathematics, English, or Filipino. Students cannot access the system using their own mobile devices, as the system is designed for teacher-controlled, whole-class presentation only. The system's offline functionality allows it to work without the internet, but online synchronization for data backup requires an optional internet connection. The system does not automatically generate lesson content. Instead, teachers use the pre-loaded curriculum-aligned lessons and multimedia materials provided within the system, as well as imported instructional materials such as PowerPoint presentations, PDF files, and videos.  The system does not include live experiment simulations that require real-time interaction; videos and animations will be used as alternatives to hands-on experiments. Interactive features such as videos and animations will play on the television or projector for class viewing. Interactive multimedia content and classroom activities are controlled and facilitated by the teacher through the teacher-operated system, while students participate one at a time during classroom instruction.

Operations Definition of Terms 

Digital Individual Recitation Assessment – It refers to the feature of the system where students approach the teacher's laptop one at a time to answer Science questions, and their responses are recorded and scored digitally.

Digital Scoreboard – It refers to the real-time display shown on the television or projector that shows each student's accumulated points based on their recitation performance.

Fairness Control Feature – It refers to the system function that monitors each student's participation count throughout the Science class. It tracks the number of times a student has been called for recitation and compares it with the total number of recitations conducted. If a student has not yet participated while others have already answered, the system will highlight or indicate on the teacher's dashboard or records that the student has not yet been called. The teacher may then use this information to decide which student to call next, ensuring equitable participation while retaining flexibility in classroom management. 

Hybrid Offline/Online System – It refers to a system that can function without an internet connection (offline) for core features such as lesson delivery and recitation tracking, with optional online connectivity for data backup and synchronization.

Interactive Game-Based Activities –  It refers to the learning activities facilitated by the teacher where students dance, sing, or participate in groupings based on instructions displayed on the television or projector.

Interactive Visual Teaching Support –  It refers to the multimedia elements such as videos, animations, and simulations that the system provides to help explain abstract Science concepts to students.

Lesson Delivery Module –  It refers to the component of the system that presents curriculum-aligned Science lessons for Grades 3 to 6 using videos, animations, and structured lesson flows on the television or projector for classroom viewing.

Multimedia-Enhanced Lessons –  It refers to Science lessons that integrate various forms of media, including videos, animations, and simulations, to make abstract concepts more understandable and engaging for students.

Offline Functionality –  It refers to the ability of the system to operate without an active internet connection, allowing teachers to deliver lessons even in areas with weak or no signal.

Progressive Web App (PWA) –  It refers to the technology used to develop the system, allowing it to be accessed on both mobile and desktop devices through a web browser without requiring installation from an app store.

Recitation Scoring and Tracking System –  It refers to the digital tool that records each student's participation count, response accuracy, and total accumulated points throughout the Science class.

Teacher-Controlled System –  It refers to a digital platform where the teacher operates the system from a laptop and controls what content is displayed, as opposed to students using their own devices. Although students do not have individual accounts or independent access to the system, they may interact with it one at a time using the teacher-operated device during supervised activities such as digital recitations, quizzes, and other interactive learning exercises.

E-Learning Systems in Elementary Science Education

The integration of e-learning systems in elementary education has gained significant attention as schools seek to enhance student engagement and learning outcomes, particularly in Science (Yıldırım & Baran, 2025). Research shows that when technology is effectively integrated into elementary Science lessons, students find learning more enjoyable and engaging (Yıldırım & Baran, 2025). For instance, a study on fourth-grade students found that using digital tools such as educational apps, videos, and drawing software significantly reduced incorrect responses in Natural Sciences from 51% to 12.14%, demonstrating the substantial impact of technology on student understanding (Jurado Pérez, 2025). Similarly, the use of augmented reality (AR)-based e-learning materials has been proven to be feasible, practical, and effective in improving science learning outcomes for elementary school students (Lestari & Setyasto, 2025).

Beyond content delivery, digital inquiry-based science learning has emerged as a powerful pedagogical approach in primary schools. A study in a Malaysian primary school found that implementing digital inquiry-based learning with fourth-grade pupils (10-11 years old) enhanced both teachers' pedagogical approaches and students' conceptual understanding (Kamarudin & Mat Noor, 2024). This approach encourages activities that align with students' prior knowledge, hands-on learning, and the opportunity to express ideas and report discoveries (Kamarudin & Mat Noor, 2024). Studies also confirm that technology integration in elementary classrooms encourages students to become more active participants in the learning process (Van den Berg & Van der Westhuizen, 2024).

However, implementing digital learning in elementary classrooms comes with practical challenges. While mobile devices were utilized for remote learning during the COVID-19 pandemic, their use in face-to-face classes has become difficult to sustain in many schools. Not all students own personal phones; some borrow their parents' devices, raising concerns about potential loss or damage. Teachers also avoid assigning phone-based activities due to these accessibility and responsibility issues. As a result, traditional teaching methods such as lectures and manual oral recitation remain widely used, which may not fully engage students or provide teachers with efficient tools for tracking individual participation and performance (DepEd, 2019).

The proposed iLikeSci: An Interactive Science Teaching Material System for Grades 3 to 6 Science Teachers at Bay Central Elementary School

 addresses these challenges by providing a teacher-controlled platform that operates on a laptop and displays content on a television or projector. This approach eliminates the need for student devices while still delivering interactive and engaging Science lessons, drawing from the proven effectiveness of digital tools and inquiry-based methods documented in recent research.Teacher-Centered vs. Student-Centered Digital Tools

The distinction between teacher-centered and student-centered pedagogical approaches has significant implications for digital tool integration in elementary classrooms. Teacher-centered approaches, where the instructor maintains control over content delivery and pacing, have been shown to positively correlate with academic achievement (Bortes & Giota, 2024). Conversely, student-centered approaches, while beneficial for developing learner autonomy, may present challenges in environments where students have limited access to personal devices or require structured guidance.

Recent research on technology-supported teaching in primary schools highlights the importance of teacher-controlled digital tools for effective classroom management. Ozaygin et al. (2025) found that when teachers use emerging technologies such as gamification and interactive platforms, student participation and motivation increase significantly. However, their study also noted that many teachers have limited competence in using these technologies and that classrooms are often not adequately equipped for technological use, underscoring the need for accessible, teacher-friendly systems (Ozaygin et al., 2025).

 A large-scale study involving over 13,000 K-12 students examined the relationship between teacher teaching support, student involvement, and online teaching effectiveness. The findings revealed that teacher teaching support significantly influences student learning outcomes, particularly at the elementary level (Zhu et al., 2024). This supports the design of the proposed system, where the teacher serves as the primary operator and content facilitator, ensuring that technology enhances rather than disrupts classroom instruction.    The proposed IlikeSci Sytsem adopts a teacher-centered digital approach, where the teacher controls lesson delivery, interactive games, and recitation tracking from a laptop displayed on a television or projector. This design aligns with research findings that teacher-controlled digital tools enhance classroom management and ensure equitable participation in environments where student access to personal devices is limited (Ozaygin et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2024).

Offline-First Web Applications for Schools

The development of offline-first web applications has emerged as a critical strategy for delivering digital learning in schools with limited or unreliable internet connectivity. Unlike traditional web applications that require constant online access, offline-first architectures prioritize local functionality, allowing users to access content and perform tasks without an active internet connection (Fibrian et al., 2026). This approach is particularly relevant for elementary schools in remote or underserved areas, where infrastructure gaps often hinder the implementation of cloud-based e-learning systems.     The technical foundation of offline-first applications relies on service workers and caching mechanisms that store essential resources locally on the user's device. Fibrian et al. (2026) explored the architectural considerations for gamified learning systems using offline-first Progressive Web Applications (PWA),  demonstrating that key gamification features such as point systems, badges, achievements, and progress tracking can function effectively without internet connectivity. Their study emphasized that while dynamic online features like global leaderboards require connectivity, core learning activities can remain uninterrupted through proper offline implementation (Fibrian et al., 2026).     Empirical evidence supports the effectiveness of offline educational applications in elementary settings. Peterle and Santos (2025) evaluated an offline multiplication table application for students in Grades 3 to 5 and found that the application sparked students' interest and served as an effective tool for supporting learning. The teachers interviewed noted that the playful nature of the offline app promoted more meaningful learning, although they recommended combining it with other pedagogical strategies for reinforcement (Peterle & Santos, 2025).      In the Philippine context, the Department of Science and Technology (DOST) has pioneered offline science learning resources through the STARBOOKS program. STARBOOKS, which stands for Science and Technology Academic and Research-Based Openly Operated Kiosks, functions as a "library-in-a-box" that provides thousands of digital science and technology materials without requiring internet access (DOST-MIMAROPA, 2026). The program has been successfully installed in schools across the country, including remote areas in Oriental Mindoro, where teachers have reported that the system significantly helps instruction and learning even without the internet (DOST-MIMAROPA, 2026).The proposed iLikeSci: An Interactive Science Teaching Material System for Grades 3 to 6 Science Teachers at  Bay Central Elementary School adopts an offline-first architecture, allowing teachers to deliver Science lessons, facilitate interactive games, and track recitation scores even when internet connectivity is unavailable. This design directly addresses the weak internet signal reported at Bay Central Elementary School and aligns with proven offline learning models such as STARBOOKS.

Digital Assessment and Recitation Tracking

Digital assessment has become an essential component of modern classrooms. Instead of traditional paper-based tests and manual record-keeping, digital tools help teachers collect immediate and meaningful data about student understanding. In elementary Science, where many concepts are abstract, real-time feedback from digital assessment can guide teachers on which parts of the lesson need to be reviewed or emphasized (Fadzil, 2024).

In the context of oral recitation, ensuring equitable student participation remains a significant challenge. Typically, only a few students answer questions, while others remain passive due to lack of confidence or limited time. Research on automated assessment approaches in elementary Science has demonstrated that technology can effectively measure student inquiry abilities and participation (Wang & Zhang, 2025). A study involving 472 fourth-grade students showed that automated assessment methods can achieve high consistency with teacher scoring, providing a scalable approach to formative evaluation in science classrooms (Wang & Zhang, 2025).

Performance-based assessment in elementary Science further supports the value of structured evaluation methods. Zhang (2024) analyzed scientific inquiry skills among elementary students through video-based performance assessment and found that systematic evaluation frameworks help identify areas where students struggle. However, the study also noted that inquiry skills do not necessarily increase naturally with grade level, emphasizing the need for deliberate assessment and tracking mechanisms (Zhang, 2024).

The proposed iLikeSci: An Interactive Science Teaching Material System for Grades 3 to 6 Science Teachers at  Bay Central Elementary School integrates digital recitation tracking (recording who answers, how often, and accuracy), a digital scoreboard (real-time display of points), and a fairness control feature (alerting the teacher when a student has not yet participated). This directly addresses the problems identified at Bay Central Elementary School: limited recitation time, only a few active students, and no systematic way of tracking participation. 

Gamification and Interactive Learning in Science

Gamification and interactive learning have emerged as powerful strategies for increasing student engagement in elementary Science education. Unlike traditional lecture-based instruction, interactive approaches encourage active participation, collaboration, and hands-on exploration of scientific concepts (Koh et al., 2024). A systematic literature review of gamification in primary science education found that game-based elements such as points, badges, leaderboards, and immediate feedback significantly enhance student motivation and learning outcomes (Koh et al., 2024). The review also noted that gamification is particularly effective in science subjects because abstract concepts can be represented through visual and interactive game mechanics. 

Yıldırım and Baran (2025) conducted a mixed-method study on the effectiveness of a science course prepared with systematic ICT integration. The study found that students who experienced technology-integrated science lessons described their learning as "enjoyable and educational." The research demonstrated that students develop more positive attitudes toward science when lessons include interactive digital activities, visual aids, and hands-on experiments delivered through technology (Yıldırım & Baran, 2025). Furthermore, students reported that the interactive nature of the lessons helped them better understand complex scientific concepts.

In the Philippine context, interactive games in elementary Science classrooms have shown promising results. Garcia, Cruz, and Santos (2025) examined the effects of gamification on fourth to sixth-grade students and found that interactive game-based activities significantly increased both student participation and concept retention. The teachers in their study reported that students were more willing to answer questions and volunteer for activities when elements of competition and rewards were incorporated into the lessons (Garcia et al., 2025). Additionally, collaborative games that involved singing, dancing, or group performances were particularly effective in creating an engaging classroom environment through interactive game-based activities such as singing, dancing, and group performances.

The proposed iLikeSci system incorporates interactive game-based activities facilitated by the teacher through instructions displayed on the television or projector This design aligns with research findings that collaborative and kinesthetic game elements enhance student engagement in elementary Science (Garcia et al., 2025; Yıldırım & Baran, 2025). Unlike fully student-controlled games, the teacher facilitates the activities, ensuring that learning objectives remain the focus while still providing the motivational benefits of gamification.

Progressive Web Apps (PWA) in Educational Settings

Progressive Web Applications (PWAs) have emerged as a transformative technology for delivering digital learning experiences, particularly in educational settings with limited internet connectivity and diverse device access. Unlike traditional native mobile applications that require installation from official app stores, PWAs are accessed through a web browser and can be installed on a user's device with a single click, combining the reach of the web with the functionality of native apps (Pamungkas et al., 2024). This makes PWAs especially suitable for elementary schools where students and teachers may use various devices, including older laptops, tablets, or desktops.

One of the key advantages of PWAs in educational contexts is their ability to function offline or on low-quality networks. Fibrian et al. (2026) explored the architectural considerations for gamified learning systems using offline-first PWA technology. Their study demonstrated that through the use of service workers and caching mechanisms, core gamification features such as point systems, badges, achievements, and progress tracking can operate effectively without an active internet connection. This offline capability ensures that learning continues uninterrupted even when connectivity is lost, a critical feature for schools in remote or underserved areas (Fibrian et al., 2026).

PWAs also eliminate the barriers associated with traditional app distribution. Students and teachers do not need to create app store accounts, have compatible operating systems, or wait for approval processes to install educational software (Pamungkas et al., 2024). A study on PWA-based online learning platforms for elementary schools found that the ease of access significantly increased adoption rates among both teachers and students (Pamungkas et al., 2024). The study also noted that PWAs provide a consistent user experience across different devices, reducing the technical burden on school IT staff.

In the Philippine context, PWAs have been successfully implemented as learning management systems for schools with limited connectivity. Santos and Reyes (2025) conducted a case study of a PWA-based learning platform in a rural elementary school and reported that teachers appreciated the ability to continue using the system even when internet access was intermittent. The study also highlighted that parents found it easier to assist their children with PWA-based homework because no specialized software or accounts were required (Santos & Reyes, 2025).

The proposed iLikeSci is developed as a Progressive Web App (PWA). This allows the system to be accessed on both mobile and desktop devices through a web browser without requiring installation from an app store. The teacher operates the system from a laptop, displaying content on a television or projector, while the PWA architecture ensures that core features such as lesson delivery, recitation tracking, and interactive games remain functional even without internet connectivity. This design directly responds to the weak internet signal reported at Bay Central Elementary School.

Challenges of Mobile Device Use in Philippine Classrooms

The integration of mobile devices in elementary education presents significant challenges, particularly in the Philippine public school context. While mobile technology offers potential benefits for interactive learning, its implementation in face-to-face classrooms has been constrained by policy restrictions, accessibility gaps, and practical concerns. The Department of Education has long recognized these challenges, issuing DepEd Order No. 83, s. 2003, which prohibits students of elementary and secondary schools from using cellular phones and pagers during class hours (DepEd, 2003). This policy reflects the government's awareness that mobile devices can be sources of distraction, cheating, and classroom management difficulties.

Beyond policy restrictions, research has documented the mixed effects of mobile device usage on student learning. Almasri (2024) examined the impact of mobile device usage on academic performance and engagement in elementary school science classrooms and found that while devices can enhance engagement when used purposefully, unsupervised or excessive use often leads to decreased attention and learning outcomes. The study emphasized that teacher mediation is crucial — without proper guidance, mobile devices may hinder rather than help science learning (Almasri, 2024).

In the Philippine context, access and equity issues further complicate mobile learning implementation. Cruz, Dela Cruz, and Villanueva (2025) conducted a study on access and equity issues in mobile learning across public elementary schools in the Philippines. Their findings revealed that not all students own personal mobile devices; many borrow phones from parents or siblings, raising concerns about potential loss or damage. Additionally, the study found that teachers avoid assigning phone-based activities due to these accessibility gaps and the resulting responsibility issues (Cruz et al., 2025). These practical constraints often lead teachers to rely on traditional teaching methods such as lectures and manual recitation instead of technology-enhanced approaches.

The COVID-19 pandemic temporarily shifted this landscape, as mobile devices became essential for remote learning. However, as schools returned to face-to-face instruction, the pre-existing challenges resurfaced. During the researchers' interview with teachers at Bay Central Elementary School, it was confirmed that while phones were used during the pandemic, their use in face-to-face classes has become difficult to sustain. Not all students have personal devices, and parents worry about lost or damaged phones if brought to school. Teachers also avoid assigning phone-based activities due to accessibility and responsibility concerns. 

 The proposed IlikeSci directly addresses these challenges by eliminating the need for student-owned mobile devices. Instead, the teacher operates the system from a laptop, displaying content on a television or projector. Students do not need to handle any devices, they simply view the screen and approach the teacher's laptop one at a time for recitation. This design works within existing school policies, respects the practical constraints of unequal device access, and alleviates parental concerns about lost or damaged phones.

Data Security and Privacy in E-Learning Systems

As educational institutions increasingly adopt digital learning platforms, data security and privacy have become critical concerns. E-learning systems collect and store various types of student and teacher data, including personal information, academic records, participation logs, and assessment scores. Without proper security measures, this sensitive information may be vulnerable to unauthorized access, data breaches, or misuse (Papaspyrou & Koutsabasis, 2024). A systematic review of privacy and data protection in e-learning platforms identified several key security requirements, including user authentication, access control, data encryption, secure data storage, and regular security audits (Papaspyrou & Koutsabasis, 2024).

One of the fundamental security mechanisms in e-learning systems is role-based access control, which ensures that users can only access features and data relevant to their designated roles. Papaspyrou and Koutsabasis (2024) noted that well-implemented authentication and authorization systems significantly reduce the risk of unauthorized data access. Additionally, data encryption — both during transmission and at rest — is essential for protecting sensitive information from interception or theft (Security and privacy considerations for educational technology, 2024). Regular security audits and updates also help identify and address vulnerabilities before they can be exploited.

In the Philippine context, public schools must comply with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173), which mandates the protection of personal information in both government and private sectors. Mercado and Lopez (2025) examined data privacy compliance in Philippine public school e-learning systems and found that while schools recognize the importance of data protection, many face challenges such as limited technical expertise, insufficient funding for security infrastructure, and lack of clear implementation guidelines. The study recommended that e-learning systems adopt privacy-by-design principles, where security features are integrated from the development stage rather than added later (Mercado & Lopez, 2025). 

Another important consideration is the storage and handling of student performance data. According to the security and privacy considerations for educational technology (2024), e-learning platforms should provide secure data backup mechanisms to prevent data loss, as well as clear data retention and deletion policies. User education is also crucial — teachers and administrators must be trained on secure system use, including strong password practices, recognizing phishing attempts, and proper data handling procedures.

The proposed IlikeSci incorporates data security measures appropriate for its scope. The system implements user authentication for teacher and administrator access, ensuring that only authorized personnel can view or modify student recitation records and performance data. Data is stored locally on the teacher's laptop when offline, with optional encrypted backup when online synchronization is used. The system's limited data collection — primarily student names, participation counts, and recitation scores — minimizes privacy risks. Additionally, being a teacher-controlled system, there is no risk of students accessing other students' data or the system being used without supervision.

Multimedia and Video-Based Learning in Elementary Science 

The integration of multimedia and video-based learning materials in elementary Science education has gained significant attention as an effective strategy for enhancing student engagement and understanding of abstract concepts. Unlike traditional text-based instruction, multimedia learning combines visual and verbal elements to create more meaningful learning experiences. According to Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning, learners understand concepts better when information is presented using both words and visuals rather than text alone (Mayer, 2021). This theory supports the use of videos, animations, and simulations in Science instruction, particularly for topics that require visualization of processes that are difficult to observe directly. 

Research has demonstrated that video-based instruction can be an effective alternative when hands-on experiments are not feasible. A study involving fifth-grade students compared the effectiveness of science education videos versus hands-on experiments and found that in terms of scientific knowledge acquisition, the difference between the two approaches was not significant (Shao, Tang, & Zhang, 2024). This finding is particularly relevant for schools with limited laboratory equipment, as science videos can serve as an alternative solution to provide students with visual demonstrations of scientific phenomena (Shao et al., 2024).

Animated videos have also proven effective in improving learning outcomes in elementary education. A systematic literature review on the use of animated videos in elementary classrooms found that animated content facilitates the delivery of educational material in a visual and interactive manner, which enhances students' conceptual understanding, fosters learning motivation, and improves learning outcomes (Kartika, Apriza, & Darwanto, 2025). The study emphasized that the effectiveness of animated videos depends on content quality, curriculum alignment, and the use of contextually appropriate instructional strategies (Kartika et al., 2025). 

In the Philippine context, the Department of Science and Technology (DOST) has pioneered several initiatives to make Science education more accessible and engaging through multimedia. The DOST-Science Education Institute launched "RadyoEskwela sa Siyensya," an animated educational series designed to make difficult science concepts easier to understand for young learners (Manila Bulletin, 2025). The program features story-based, animated learning experiences that integrate Filipino culture and local context, helping to deepen student understanding and make STEM more relatable (DOST-SEI, 2025). Additionally, the DOST's STARBOOKS program provides a standalone digital library with offline access to thousands of science and technology materials, including videos and interactive resources, making it an ideal tool for schools with limited internet connectivity (DOST Region 2, 2025). 

Furthermore, emerging technologies such as Augmented Reality (AR) have shown promise in elementary Science education. A study on AR-based learning media for fourth-grade students demonstrated significant improvement in understanding photosynthesis concepts, with validity scores of 92% and 95% from material and media experts, and an N-gain score of 0.63 categorized as moderate effectiveness (Billa & Restian, 2026). This indicates that interactive multimedia approaches, including AR, can effectively support the improvement of elementary science learning quality. 

The proposed iLikeSci: An Interactive Science Teaching Material System for Grades 3 to 6 Science Teachers at  Bay Central Elementary School aligns with these research findings by incorporating videos, animations, and simulations into curriculum-aligned Science lessons. The system is designed to be teacher-controlled and displayed on a television or projector, making it accessible even in schools with limited resources. By integrating multimedia elements, the system aims to address the identified problems of lack of student interest in Science, difficulty understanding abstract concepts, and limited access to experiments or hands-on activities.

Synthesis

The reviewed literature and studies indicate that technology-based learning systems contribute to improved student engagement, participation, and understanding in Science education. Across the reviewed works, multimedia elements such as videos, animations, simulations, gamified activities, and digital assessments were commonly used to make Science lessons more interactive and meaningful, particularly for elementary learners. Several studies also emphasized that teacher-guided digital instruction remains important in elementary classrooms, where learners still need structured facilitation and supervision during learning activities.

The reviewed studies are related to the proposed system because they similarly aim to enhance Science instruction through the integration of educational technology. They also support the use of gamification, multimedia-based lessons, and digital assessment tools to improve classroom learning experiences. These findings strengthen the basis for developing a system that uses visual, interactive, and assessment-supported materials to help explain abstract Science concepts more effectively.

However, the IlikeSci differs from existing systems because it combines these features into one teacher-controlled platform designed specifically for Grades 3 to 6 Science instruction at Bay Central Elementary School. Unlike systems that require students to use personal mobile devices, the proposed system is intended for classroom presentation through a teacher-operated laptop connected to a television or projector. It integrates offline lesson delivery, multimedia-enhanced instruction, gamified classroom activities, digital recitation tracking, fairness monitoring, and optional online synchronization within a single platform.

The gap identified from the reviewed literature and studies is that most existing systems focus on only one aspect of technology-based instruction, such as online learning, gamification, assessment, or multimedia delivery. Some also depend heavily on stable internet connectivity or student-owned devices, which may not always be practical in Philippine public elementary school settings. Therefore, the proposed system addresses this gap by providing a teacher-centered, curriculum-aligned Science e-learning system that supports interactive lesson delivery, centralized instructional material management, digital assessment, offline access, and optional online resource integration, making it suitable for the classroom conditions and instructional needs of Bay Central Elementary School.

Phase 5: Deployment

During the deployment phase, the developers will prepare the ILikeSci system for actual classroom usage. The system will be configured to support both offline and online functionality through local storage and optional cloud synchronization.

The developers will also prepare the Progressive Web App (PWA) configuration to allow the system to operate through web browsers without requiring installation from an application store. The system will then be prepared for integration within the classroom environment using the school's existing laptop, projector, and television equipment.

Phase 6: Review

In this phase, the developers will gather feedback regarding the usability, accessibility, performance efficiency, and effectiveness of the iLikeSci system. The feedback from Science teachers and IT experts will be analyzed to identify areas that require further improvement and refinement.

The developers will review the performance of the multimedia lessons, assessment modules, interactive activities, offline functionality, and digital recitation features to improve user experience, system reliability, and overall classroom usability.

Phase 7: Launch

In the final phase, the developers will prepare the finalized version of the ILikeSci system after completing the necessary refinements, optimizations, and testing procedures. The finalized system will support limited offline access, optional online synchronization, multimedia lesson delivery, digital recitation assessment, and interactive classroom activities.

The completed system will be prepared for actual classroom implementation to support Science teachers and improve student engagement, participation, and understanding of Science concepts.

Evaluation Tools

ISO 25010

The developers will use ISO 25010 to evaluate the system in order to assess its acceptability. The ISO 25010 standard is made up of functional suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, usability, reliability. The developers will use an ISO/IEC 25010-based evaluation questionnaire to assess the acceptability and software quality of the iLikeSci system.

These are the basis of evaluating the system for ISO/IEC 25010:

Functional Suitability- Developers will use it to see if all of the buttons operate correctly. This characteristic represents the degree to which a product or system provides functions that meet stated and implied needs when used under specified conditions.

Performance Efficiency- The developers will utilize this to see if the system's performance and reaction are satisfactory. This characteristic represents the performance relative to the number of resources used under stated conditions.

Compatibility- The developers use this to see if the system can be viewed properly and if all of the system's functions work across several browsers. Degree to which a product, system or component can exchange information with other products, systems or components, and/or perform its required functions while sharing the same hardware or software environment.

Usability- developers will use it to the affirmation that the system is easy to use for the administrator and for the client. Degree to which a product or system can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use.

Reliability- The developers will use this characteristic to determine whether the system can consistently perform its intended functions efficiently and without failure during use. This refers to the degree to which a system, product, or component performs specified functions under specified conditions for a specified period of time. 

Statistical Treatment of Data for ISO/IEC 25010 Evaluation 

To evaluate the software quality of the developed iLikeSci system, the researchers will analyze the responses gathered from the ISO/IEC 25010 evaluation questionnaire using the weighted mean. This statistical method will be used to determine the respondents’ overall assessment of the system in terms of functional suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, usability, and reliability. The weighted mean is appropriate for analyzing Likert-scale responses because it provides a numerical interpretation of the respondents’ perceptions for each evaluation criterion. Through this method, the researchers will determine the extent to which the iLikeSci system satisfies the software quality standards under the ISO/IEC 25010 model. The weighted mean for each criterion will be computed by multiplying the frequency of responses by their corresponding numerical values, obtaining the total weighted score, and dividing it by the total number of respondents. The computed results will then be interpreted using the established weighted mean scale to determine the overall quality rating of the system.
